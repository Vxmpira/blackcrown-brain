<h1>ICT Kill Zones: What They Are and How Traders Use Them</h1>

<p class="lede">A kill zone is a window of time that ICT-style traders watch closely. The idea is simple: price does not move the same way all day. Some hours carry more volume, more reaction, and more order flow than others.</p>

<p>This guide explains the term, shows the windows most often taught, and walks through how to check the times yourself. It is education, not a trading plan. Nothing here says a window will produce a profit.</p>

<h2>The short definition</h2>
<p>"Kill zone" is a label from Inner Circle Trader (ICT) material. It describes a session window where price action is often more active. Traders use these windows to decide when to pay attention, not to decide what to buy or sell.</p>

<h2>The windows most often taught</h2>
<p>Sources disagree on exact times. The list below reflects common ICT teaching. Treat it as a starting point and verify against a live calendar before you rely on any of it.</p>

<div class="zones">
<div class="zone"><strong>Asia kill zone.</strong> Evening into the early overnight hours, Eastern Time. Often used to mark a range that later sessions react to.</div>
<div class="zone"><strong>London kill zone.</strong> Early morning in New York time. Frequently described as the first major liquidity event of the day.</div>
<div class="zone"><strong>New York kill zone.</strong> The morning US open. Index futures see heavy participation, so many traders watch this window most closely.</div>
<div class="zone"><strong>London close.</strong> Late morning into midday. Some traders look for reversals or cleanup moves here.</div>
</div>

<h2>Why traders care about timing</h2>
<p>Index futures trade nearly around the clock, but activity is uneven. Volume rises when major financial centers open. Bigger participants tend to act at these times, so price often moves with more force and more clear structure.</p>
<p>The kill zone idea says: know when the market is busiest, and watch the behavior in those windows. That is the whole concept. It does not replace risk management or a process of your own.</p>

<h2>How to check the times yourself</h2>
<p>Session windows are only useful if you know them exactly. Daylight saving changes shift the clock twice a year, so a window that lines up in January may be off by an hour in July.</p>
<p>Read the full breakdown of futures session times in Eastern Time on our <a href="/p/futures-session-times-et">futures session times guide</a>. Convert every window to your own timezone before you build a watchlist from it.</p>

<h2>Kill zones and resting liquidity</h2>
<p>Many ICT lessons connect session windows to liquidity. Resting orders sit above highs and below lows, and traders talk about those pools as targets. Learn how that idea works in our <a href="/p/resting-liquidity">resting liquidity guide</a> before you try to combine it with session timing.</p>

<h2>A simple way to start</h2>
<p>Keep it small. Pick one window. Mark the session open on your chart. Watch what price does for a few weeks without placing a single trade based on it. Write down what you saw. That exercise teaches more than any rule copied from someone else.</p>
