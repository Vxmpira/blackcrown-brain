<h1>Liquidity Sweeps Explained for Index Futures Traders</h1>
<p class="lede">Price does not always move because of news. Sometimes it moves to a place where a lot of orders are waiting. Traders call that move a liquidity sweep. This page explains the idea in plain language, with the vocabulary you will hear on trading desks, and no promise of any result.</p>

<h2>What resting liquidity is</h2>
<p>Resting liquidity is orders sitting in the book that have not filled yet. In index futures, many of those orders cluster in predictable places. Stop orders are the largest group. A trader who is long often places a stop below a recent low. A trader who is short often places a stop above a recent high. Breakout traders place buy stops just above obvious highs.</p>
<p>Each of those orders is a standing instruction. When price reaches the level, the order becomes a market order. That is liquidity. It is the fuel a larger move needs to fill size. Without resting orders, a big participant has nobody to trade against.</p>

<h2>Why price runs stops</h2>
<p>A large participant who wants to buy many contracts needs sellers. Sellers are easiest to find where sell stops sit, below prior lows, because those stops become market sells once triggered. Pushing price down through a prior low can fill buy size against that forced flow.</p>
<p>The same logic works in reverse above prior highs. ICT-informed traders describe this as price seeking liquidity. The plain version is simpler. Price travels to levels where orders are stacked, trades through them, and sometimes keeps going.</p>
<p>Honest caveat: nobody can see one firm's intent from a chart. You see a footprint. Price wicks through a level, and then something happens after it. The story about why is an interpretation.</p>

<h2>What a sweep looks like</h2>
<p>A sweep is usually a short trade through a visible level, followed by a fast return. The level is often a prior day high or low, a prior week extreme, or a session high or low. The wick is the evidence. The close back inside the old range is the part many traders watch most closely.</p>

<h2>Reversal or continuation</h2>
<p>A sweep is a fact about price, not a verdict. Price trades through a level where stops sat, then returns or keeps going. The return is the part that matters for interpretation.</p>
<ul>
<li>If price takes a high, closes back below it, and keeps falling, many traders read a failed breakout. The sweep marked a possible reversal.</li>
<li>If price takes a high and keeps accelerating through it, the sweep was only a stop run inside a continuation.</li>
</ul>
<p>Both can look identical for the first few bars. That is why context matters. Traders weigh the higher timeframe direction, the session timing, and whether the move came with real participation or thin trading.</p>

<h2>What traders watch when a level is taken</h2>
<p>These are things experienced traders check. None of them is a signal, and none of them works every time.</p>
<ul>
<li>Was the level visible before the move? Obvious levels attract more stops.</li>
<li>Did the wick pierce the level and close back inside?</li>
<li>How did the next few candles behave? Did structure shift in the other direction?</li>
<li>Did the move happen during a busy session, around a scheduled release, or in quiet trade?</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common beginner mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Calling every wick a sweep. Most wicks are noise.</li>
<li>Ignoring the timeframe. A sweep on a one minute chart can sit inside a larger trend.</li>
<li>Assuming a sweep guarantees a reversal. It does not.</li>
<li>Drawing levels after the move, then claiming they were obvious.</li>
</ul>
