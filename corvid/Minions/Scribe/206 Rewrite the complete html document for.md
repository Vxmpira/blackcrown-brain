  <h2>The four windows</h2>
  <p>ICT teaching names these windows as killzones. Start and end times vary a little between sources, so treat the ranges below as the common reference points. Check your own clock against them.</p>

  <div class="grid">
    <div class="card">
      <h3>Asia</h3>
      <p class="time">20:00 to 00:00 ET</p>
      <p>Often a quieter range. Price builds levels that later sessions may test. Thin liquidity can make moves look bigger than they are.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="card">
      <h3>London</h3>
      <p class="time">02:00 to 05:00 ET</p>
      <p>European desks open and reprice the overnight range. This is often where the first real directional push of the day shows up.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="card">
      <h3>New York AM</h3>
      <p class="time">07:00 to 10:00 ET</p>
      <p>US cash volume arrives. Economic releases and the open tend to concentrate activity here, and many traders watch this window most closely.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="card">
      <h3>New York PM</h3>
      <p class="time">13:30 to 16:00 ET</p>
      <p>The afternoon session. Trend continuation or reversal can develop as the day matures and the closing auction approaches.</p>
    </div>
  </div>

  <h2>Why volume clusters here</h2>
  <p>Volume does not gather in these windows by accident. Three forces drive most of it.</p>
  <p>First, market hours. When a major financial center is open, its participants trade. London and New York overlap with the most liquid index futures activity of the day.</p>
  <p>Second, scheduled information. Economic data, central bank remarks, and earnings tend to land at fixed times. Each release forces repricing, and repricing pulls volume.</p>
  <p>Third, resting orders. Stops, limit orders, and prior session levels sit at known prices. When price reaches them during an active window, the orders execute together and the move looks sharp.</p>
