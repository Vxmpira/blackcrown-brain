  <h2>1. Scheduled data releases</h2>
  <p>Employment, inflation, growth and manufacturing numbers hit at fixed times. The market prices expectations before the print. The surprise is what moves price, not the number alone. A strong reading that was already expected can do very little. A weak reading in a market that was leaning the other way can move fast.</p>
  <p>What to watch: the consensus, the prior reading, and the gap between them. Revisions matter too.</p>

  <h2>2. Central bank talk</h2>
  <p>Rate decisions get the headlines. Speeches, minutes and press conference wording often move futures more. Traders listen for shifts in tone: a word that softens or hardens, a new emphasis on jobs over prices. Small changes in language can reprice the path of rates for months.</p>

  <h2>3. Headlines</h2>
  <p>Geopolitics, earnings from large index components, trade policy and surprise announcements arrive without warning. Headlines matter most when they change expectations, not when they simply repeat what the market already knows. Many headlines are noise. Some are not. The job is telling them apart quickly.</p>

  <h2>4. Liquidity</h2>
  <p>Liquidity decides how far a move can travel. Thin conditions around the open, the close, or overnight sessions can exaggerate a headline. Deep conditions absorb it. Session levels, prior day ranges and the position of resting orders often explain why a move stalls or runs.</p>
