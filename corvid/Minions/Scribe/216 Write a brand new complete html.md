<h1>Killzones: Session Windows for Index Futures Traders</h1>
<p>A killzone is a fixed window of the trading day when a session is most active. Index futures traders watch these windows because volume and volatility tend to cluster inside them. This page explains what killzones are, why they get attention, and how prior session levels give them context.</p>
<p>This is education, not a forecast. No window guarantees a move, and no level guarantees a reaction.</p>

<h2>What a killzone is</h2>
<p>The term comes from the ICT concepts community. It describes time blocks, usually defined in New York time, when liquidity from a major financial center is most likely to be present. A killzone is a time label. It does not tell you what price will do.</p>
<p>Exact start and end times differ from source to source. Always confirm the window in your own chart timezone settings before you use it.</p>

<h2>Why index futures traders watch them</h2>
<p>Index futures trade nearly around the clock, but activity is uneven. Traders watch killzones for three reasons:</p>
<ul>
<li>Liquidity tends to concentrate when major sessions open and overlap.</li>
<li>Volatility often changes character when a new session becomes active.</li>
<li>Time blocks give a repeatable frame for reviewing what the market did, which is useful for study.</li>
</ul>
<p>Liquidity and volatility are observations, not promises. A busy window can still chop sideways.</p>

<h2>How prior session highs and lows frame them</h2>
<p>Many traders mark the prior session high and low before a killzone opens. These levels describe where the market has already been. They are reference points, not targets.</p>
<p>Common context marks include the prior day high and low, the prior week high and low, and the overnight range built before the New York open. Watching how price interacts with these levels inside a window is a study exercise. It is not a signal.</p>
