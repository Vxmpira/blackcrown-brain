      <h1>Futures trading sessions, explained</h1>
      <p class="lede">Index futures trade almost around the clock. The day is split into sessions tied to the big financial centers of Asia, Europe and North America. This guide covers when each one opens, what it tends to look like, and why the overlaps matter.</p>

      <h2>What a session means</h2>
      <p>A session is a window of time when a particular group of traders is most active. Nothing switches off at the boundary. Orders keep flowing, but the mix of participants changes, and so does how much volume moves through the market.</p>
      <p>Sessions are useful as context. They help you ask the right question when you look at a chart: who is likely trading right now, and how much activity is there to read?</p>

    <h2>The three main sessions</h2>
    <div class="cards">
      <div class="card asia">
        <h3>Asia</h3>
        <p>Often opens with Tokyo and Sydney. For US index futures it is frequently the quietest of the three, with narrower ranges. That is a tendency, not a rule.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card london">
        <h3>London</h3>
        <p>European money arrives and volume often rises. Early direction set here can carry into the US open, or reverse once New York joins.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card ny">
        <h3>New York</h3>
        <p>The US cash open brings the heaviest index activity, and most scheduled US economic releases land here. Moves can be fast and wide.</p>

    <h2>Why the overlap gets so much attention</h2>
    <p class="prose">When London and New York are both open, more participants are trading at once. That usually means more volume and more traffic through the same prices. It also means moves can be quicker. The overlap is where most traders look first, and where a lot of noise lives too.</p>

    <h2>How to use session context</h2>
    <div class="note-box prose">
      <p>Session context describes who is trading and how busy the market is. It does not tell you what price will do next. Treat sessions as a lens for reading the chart, not a signal to act.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="prose">
      <p>A few habits that beginners find useful:</p>
      <p>Write down the session before you read a chart, so you know the backdrop. Compare how the Asia range held up when London opened. Notice whether the New York open continued or reversed the morning. Keep a simple log of what you saw, not what you hoped would happen.
