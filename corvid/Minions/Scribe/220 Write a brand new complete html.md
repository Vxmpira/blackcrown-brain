  <h1>Liquidity Sweeps in Index Futures</h1>
  <p class="lede">A liquidity sweep is price moving through a visible level to reach the orders resting beyond it. It is one of the most common moves in index futures, and one of the most misread. This page explains what a sweep is, where the liquidity sits, and how to study the pattern on your own charts.</p>

  <h2>What a liquidity sweep is</h2>
  <p>Resting orders cluster at obvious places. Stop orders sit just beyond recent swing highs and swing lows. Limit orders stack at round numbers and at prior session extremes. When price trades through one of these clusters, the resting orders get filled, and the move that did it is called a sweep, or a stop run.</p>
  <p>A sweep is a description of what price did, not a promise of what comes next. The same wick through a level can be followed by a reversal, a continuation, or a chop. The useful work is in the context around the sweep, and in the discipline of testing your reading against many examples.</p>

  <h2>Where the liquidity sits</h2>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Equal highs and equal lows.</strong> Two or more swing points at nearly the same price. Many traders place stops just beyond them.</li>
    <li><strong>Prior day high and low.</strong> Widely watched by participants across sessions, so orders gather there.</li>
    <li><strong>Session highs and lows.</strong> The Asia, London, and New York ranges each leave their own extremes on the chart.</li>
    <li><strong>Prior week high and low.</strong> Larger levels, often visible long before price returns to them.</li>
    <li><strong>Round numbers.</strong> Whole and half thousand levels in index futures attract resting orders on both sides.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>The sequence most traders describe looks like this:</p>
  <ol>
    <li>Price builds a visible level, such as two swing highs at nearly the same price.</li>
    <li>Price trades through that level, often briefly, and prints a wick above it.</li>
    <li>The candle closes back inside the prior range. This close is the detail most people skip.</li>
    <li>The next several candles show whether the market accepts the new price or rejects it.</li>
  </ol>
  <p>A wick that closes back inside is a fact. Whether it leads anywhere is a question you answer with more data, not with one example.</p>

  <h2>A study checklist for sweeps</h2>
  <dl>
    <dt>Which level was taken?</dt>
    <dd>Name it before you look at the outcome. Equal highs, prior day, session range, and round numbers behave differently.</dd>
    <dt>How far did price go?</dt>
    <dd>Measure the wick in points and compare it with recent range. A shallow poke and a deep flush are not the same event.</dd>
    <dt>Where did it close?</dt>
    <dd>Note whether the candle closed back inside the level or held beyond it.</dd>
    <dt>What was the session context?</dt>
    <dd>Sweeps near the open, near economic releases, or into a session close carry different participation.</dd>
    <dt>What happened next?</dt>
    <dd>Log the next twenty bars. Keep the log whether the result agrees with your idea or not.</dd>
  </dl>

  <h2>Questions</h2>
  <dl>
    <dt>Is a sweep the same as a breakout?</dt>
    <dd>Not always. A breakout is accepted beyond the level. A sweep is a move through the level that often closes back inside it. The close and the following bars separate the two.</dd>
    <dt>Do sweeps only happen at big levels?</dt>
    <dd>No. Small intraday highs and lows get swept constantly. Larger levels are simply watched by more participants.</dd>
    <dt>Can I trade sweeps mechanically?</dt>
